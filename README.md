Open a coupon site before you check out and you'll get a wall of codes. Most of them won't work. A few will, and one of those few can quietly cost you more than it saves, because the coupon aggregation industry and the cashback industry fight over the same commission — and you're the one holding the receipt when it goes wrong.

That last part isn't a conspiracy theory. It's how last-click attribution works, and it's the single most useful thing to understand before you type anything into a promo field again.

## The real reason your coupon code "doesn't work"

Codes die fast. A merchant runs a 15% off promotion for a weekend, the code gets scraped by a dozen aggregators, the sale ends, and the code sits on those pages for another eight months looking perfectly valid. You paste it, the field turns red, you shrug and pay full price. That's the boring failure.

The expensive failure is different. When several marketing links point at the same purchase, the last one clicked before checkout gets credited with the sale. That's the standard referral rule across affiliate networks. Copy a code from a coupon site and often you're also carrying that site's referral link in your browser session. Complete the purchase and the commission goes to the coupon site instead of the cashback platform you started from — which means the cashback you were counting on doesn't get tracked at all.

Coupon browser extensions do a version of this too. They pop up at the payment step, you click "apply," and they inject their own referral on the way through.

So the question isn't only "does this code work?" It's "does this code work *without* wiping out the rest of what I was going to earn?" Those are different questions, and almost no coupon page answers the second one.

## The three layers, and who pays for each

Here's the thing that makes coupon codes less of a zero-sum game than they look. On a single order you can collect three separate discounts, and they don't compete because they come out of three different budgets.

| Layer | What it does | Who funds it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Promo code | Cuts the price at checkout | The merchant's retail margin |
| Card rewards | Points, miles, or statement credit | Your card issuer |
| Cashback | A percentage of what you pay, returned after the order confirms | The merchant's affiliate commission budget |

A merchant funds a discount out of its own margin. It funds cashback out of its marketing budget. Your card issuer funds rewards out of its own economics. Nobody is paying twice for the same dollar, so nothing cancels out — unless you break the tracking that connects the layers.

## Which coupon codes are safe to use

The rule that survives contact with reality is source discipline. Three sources are generally clean:

**Codes published by the merchant itself.** Sign-up newsletters, the merchant's own banner, a code shown in your account. These are funded by the merchant and don't carry an outside referral.

**Codes listed on the cashback platform's merchant page.** These have been checked to coexist with tracking, which is exactly why the platform bothers to list them. **Codes applied automatically by the cashback platform's own extension.** Same logic — the extension is the referring party, so there's nothing to overwrite.

Anything else is a gamble. Occasionally the gamble wins: a 30% off code from an unknown site can beat a 4% cashback rate, and if you're spending $200 the math is obvious. But you have to actually run the math, and you have to accept that you're choosing one layer instead of three.

## What kills a cashback claim

- An outside coupon code or coupon extension taking the last click. The most common cause by a distance.
- Ad blockers and content blockers interfering with the tracking cookie.
- Switching devices, browsers, or apps between the click-through and the payment.
- Starting the purchase in the merchant's own app after activating on desktop.
- Gift cards. Buying them and paying with them are both typically excluded from affiliate commission structures, so neither earns cashback and the balance paid by card earns proportionally less.
- Returns, cancellations, exchanges, and partial refunds. Cashback is paid on completed sales, so a return reverses it.
- Excluded categories and line items. Fees, taxes, service charges, and shipping don't earn, and neither do voucher or store-credit redemptions.

ShopBack's own support page describes filing a missing cashback claim with the order number, order date and time, subtotal, and — this matters — whether you used a coupon code and where you found it. That last question exists for a reason.

## Questions that come up repeatedly

**Is a coupon code worth more than cashback?**
Usually yes, when the discount is large. A code comes off the price immediately and in full; cashback is a percentage of what remains, paid after confirmation. A one-off 20% code beats a 4% cashback rate comfortably. For small discounts, the platform-listed code plus cashback usually wins, because the outside code costs you the cashback.

**Can I use both?**
Yes, when the code comes from the merchant or from the cashback platform's store page. No, when it comes from a random coupon site — that's the version that breaks tracking.

**Does a coupon reduce my cashback?**
It reduces the base. Cashback is calculated on what you actually pay, so a discount lowers the cashback amount slightly. The discount is normally much bigger than the reduction.

**Do all cards work?**
Cashback tracks from the click-through, not from your card, so it works with whatever you pay with. Card rewards depend on your own card's programme.

**Is any of this against the rules?**
No. Each layer is funded by a party that intends to pay it. Stacking is just using each programme the way it was built.

**What if it doesn't track?**
File a missing cashback claim inside your account with the order number, date, subtotal, and coupon details. Start there rather than with a support email.
